Finding an image in a browser does not automatically give you permission to publish it. Search engines help locate images; the rights and conditions come from the image's creator or the site that hosts it.

Google's own guidance says to use its Usage rights filter as a starting point, then check the license details with the provider and host. Search labels can be incomplete or wrong, so the source page matters.

## A safe image-search workflow

1. **Open the original source page.** Do not treat a search thumbnail or a `gstatic` preview URL as the original file.
2. **Read the exact license.** Check whether commercial use and modification are allowed, and whether attribution or share-alike terms apply.
3. **Record the details.** Save the creator, source URL, license name, and any required credit beside the asset or in the article.
4. **Download from the authorized source.** Keep a local optimized copy only when the license permits it; otherwise link to or embed it according to the host's terms.
5. **Use your own artwork when licensing is unclear.** A simple original illustration is safer than an attractive image with unknown rights.

Creative Commons licenses are not all identical. Some require attribution, some restrict commercial use, and some require adaptations to use the same license. CC0 is a public-domain dedication, but even then check for other rights such as recognizable people, trademarks, or property.

## Avoid hotlinking search thumbnails

Thumbnail URLs are not a stable publishing source. They may stop working, change size, or point to a provider's preview rather than the licensed original. For a blog cover, keep a properly licensed, optimized asset with a clear filename and meaningful alt text—or create an original graphic.

The practical rule is simple: search for images, but verify permission at the source before publishing.
