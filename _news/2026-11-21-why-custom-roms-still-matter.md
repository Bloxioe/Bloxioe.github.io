Buying a phone should mean owning the hardware in your hand, not renting permission to run software on it.

## What's happening

Most Android smartphones ship with a built-in expiration date. After two or three years, official OS updates stop, background telemetry stays active, and perfectly capable hardware is pushed toward the drawer. At the same time, tighter platform policies are making it harder to install independent apps outside official stores.

Custom ROMs like LineageOS, GrapheneOS, and /e/OS exist to solve both problems at once.

## What open Android gives users

Switching away from stock firmware isn't just for tinkerers — it changes how long and how safely a device can be used:

- **Extended hardware life**: older devices continue receiving current Android security patches years after vendor support ends.
- **Control over background services**: you decide whether Google Play Services is installed, sandboxed, or left off completely.
- **Unrestricted app installation**: sideloading open-source tools from F-Droid or direct GitHub releases works without external gatekeeping.

## Three things to check before flashing

1. **Bootloader unlock policy.** Always verify whether your device model allows clean bootloader unlocking without voiding essential hardware features.
2. **Active maintainer support.** Stick to builds with regular monthly security merges rather than abandoned forum ports.
3. **Full local backup.** Keep your photos, keys, and two-factor tokens backed up offline before wiping partitions.

## Why this matters

Open ecosystems survive because alternatives remain practical. Supporting open Android builds keeps pressure on manufacturers and ensures users always have an exit ramp when default policies go too far.
